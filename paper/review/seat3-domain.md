## Reviewer 3 — Domain: Covert Channels & Security

**Summary assessment**

For a 3-day sprint output, the paper is unusually literate in the classical covert-channel tradition: it correctly types its object as a *storage* channel, grounds it in Lampson's confinement problem, Kemmerer's shared-resource matrix, TCSEC's separation of covert-channel analysis from access control, and Goguen–Meseguer non-interference, and it draws the single most important distinction correctly — the flow is covert with respect to a non-interference *policy* but not with respect to file-system *permissions*, which is exactly why authorization controls are blind to it. The co-tenant vs. cross-tenant threat-model separation (the axis on which it distinguishes itself from AIS-14) is handled cleanly and consistently. The framing is sound and the authors are refreshingly honest about the reach of their own novelty (they concede Metere's methodological priority and claim no novelty for capacity measurement per se). The two real domain weaknesses are: (1) the central "no published control governs this" claim is over-broad because it never engages the information-flow-control (IFC/DIFC) systems literature, which is precisely the published control class for flow through permitted operations and the ancestor of the paper's own namespace remedy; and (2) several canonical references a covert-channel referee would expect are absent, and a few conceptual moves (measured-zero vs. proven non-interference; zero-error vs. Shannon capacity; timing channels out of scope; the "report the residual not the fraction" convention presented as a correction rather than as re-imported classical practice) need tightening. None is fatal; all are addressable in text.

**Genuine strengths**

- The policy-relativity of "covert" is stated correctly and used well (lines 142–151): the channel is covert w.r.t. non-interference, not w.r.t. permissions, and the remedy (per-agent namespaces) turns the flow into "an ordinary, visible, denied access." This is the crux insight and it is right.
- The storage-channel characterization is correct: every carrier (filename, dirname, existence, size, mtime) is an *attribute* of a shared resource the sender modifies and the receiver references — textbook Kemmerer. The `order` row (0 bits because `list_dir` sorts) is a genuinely instructive demonstration of why declared field widths ($\log_2(n!)=1684$) are not capacity.
- Achievable-rate-by-working-encoder under a zero-error criterion is the methodologically honest way to report covert-channel capacity, and the paper correctly notes this is conservative and lower-bounds mutual information (lines 199–204).
- The co-tenant/cross-tenant distinction from AIS-14 is drawn crisply and consistently; the paper does not overclaim that existing controls fail at cross-tenant leakage.
- The cost inversion (the *structural* control that reaches zero is cheaper than the *payload* control that does not) is a genuine, non-obvious finding, not a re-description.

**Issues**

- **MAJOR — Central novelty claim vs. the IFC/DIFC literature — Intro (lines 84–99, 96–99) and Background (lines 133–164).** The framing that authorization controls are "constitutively blind" and that "this is the classical residual that access control never covered" is correct *only* if "access control" excludes information-flow control. Decentralized Information Flow Control and the systems built on it are precisely the published control class designed to govern flow through *permitted* operations, including metadata/storage channels, and the paper's proposed remedy (per-agent namespaces) is a coarse compartmentalization instance of exactly that lineage. Not engaging it leaves the central claim over-broad and the remedy under-credited. *Fix:* scope the claim to "no published control **for agentic evaluation infrastructure**" and add a short paragraph acknowledging IFC as the general-purpose ancestor of the namespace remedy, explaining why full DIFC is not proposed (weight/impracticality for eval harnesses; namespaces are the pragmatic subset). Cite Myers & Liskov, "A Decentralized Model for Information Flow Control," SOSP 1997; Zeldovich et al., "Making Information Flow Explicit in HiStar," OSDI 2006 (explicitly built to minimize covert *storage* channels); optionally Krohn et al. "Information Flow Control for Standard OS Abstractions" (Flume), SOSP 2007.

- **MAJOR — AIS-11 coverage vs. verification, stated inconsistently — Intro (lines 84–93), Discussion (lines 1378–1383).** AIS-11 by the paper's own quote governs "inter-agent message passing and **memory sharing**," and two co-tenant agents sharing a cache *is* inter-agent memory sharing — so AIS-11 nominally *covers* this flow; the real gap is that it "attaches no method for verifying it" (which the paper elsewhere says, e.g., line 92, 1637–1640). The manuscript oscillates between the strong claim (the flow is ungoverned) and the correct weaker one (it is governed as aspiration without a verification method). *Fix:* commit throughout to the weaker, defensible framing — the contribution is a *verification method for an under-specified control*, not the discovery of an ungoverned flow — and remove or soften "constitutively blind"/"never covered" phrasings so they do not contradict the AIS-11 memory-sharing quote.

- **MAJOR — Measured-zero is not proven non-interference — Table 1 (line 393), Discussion (lines 1378–1390), Conclusion.** The paper claims dirname closure "reaches 0" and that content inspection "does not discharge a non-interference requirement," implying the zero rung discharges it. But non-interference is an all-quantified proof obligation (no encoder exists), whereas an empirical 0 from one encoder over 8 trials is a sampling result — exactly the "closed channel vs. broken encoder" ambiguity the paper itself raises in §3.3. For the zero rung the paper should give a *structural* argument (once neither file nor directory names are sender-chosen, and existence presupposes sender-named slots, no sender-choosable attribute remains, so the flow is provably zero), paralleling the structural argument it already makes for content in §5.3. As written, the leap from "measured 0 bits" to "reaches zero / non-interference" overstates what one encoder's failure establishes. *Fix:* add the structural non-existence argument for the zero rung; distinguish "measured 0" from "provably non-interfering" explicitly.

- **MINOR/MODERATE — Missing canonical covert-channel-analysis reference — Background §2.2 and Method §3.** The paper's entire method (enumerate shared attributes, exhibit encoders, report residual *bandwidth in bits*) re-implements the canonical covert-channel-analysis methodology, and the "report the residual, not the fraction removed" convention (§4.7, lines 925–951) is in fact the *established* TCSEC/NCSC practice — bandwidth thresholds (e.g., the 1 and 100 bits/sec lines) were always absolute, never fractional. Presenting residual-reporting as a correction to bad practice, without crediting that absolute-bandwidth reporting is the classical norm, will read to a domain referee as re-discovering standard practice. *Fix:* cite NCSC-TG-030, "A Guide to Understanding Covert Channel Analysis of Trusted Systems" (1993), and reframe §4.7 as *re-importing* the classical absolute-bandwidth convention into the AI-eval setting (where fraction-removed is the local error), rather than as a novel corrective.

- **MINOR/MODERATE — "Co-tenant" terminology collides with cloud co-residency — throughout; define at first use (line 65, §3 line 221).** In security usage "co-tenant" ordinarily means *different* tenants co-located on shared hardware (i.e., a trust boundary between them) — the Ristenpart et al. co-residency setting. This paper uses it for *same-tenant, different-episode* agents, the opposite trust posture. The term is defined (line 65, "separate sandboxes under a single tenant") but the collision invites misreading, and a referee will expect one sentence distinguishing this from cloud co-residency covert/side channels. *Fix:* add a distinguishing sentence and cite Ristenpart, Tromer, Shacham & Savage, "Hey, You, Get Off of My Cloud," CCS 2009, noting that work is cross-trust and side-channel (timing), whereas this is intra-trust and a storage channel. Consider "intra-tenant / same-principal, multi-agent" as a clarifying gloss.

- **MINOR/MODERATE — Timing channels not scoped out — §3 threat model, §4.1.** Classical covert-channel analysis enumerates *timing* channels alongside storage; this paper measures only stored attributes (including `mtime`, a stored timestamp, not a timing channel) and shows `order` is unobservable, but never addresses wall-clock timing, cache read-latency, or lock/contention channels. A domain referee will notice the enumeration is storage-only. *Fix:* one explicit scope sentence in §3 stating that timing channels (latency, contention, real-time ordering) are out of scope and why (the substrate exposes no sender-controllable timing observable to the receiver across episode boundaries), so the enumeration is not mistaken for exhaustive.

- **MINOR — Zero-error vs. Shannon capacity precision — §3.2 (lines 322–337), §2.3 (lines 199–204).** "8/8 exact recovery" is an empirical proxy for a *zero-error* rate (Shannon's $C_0$), which is a distinct and generally smaller quantity than ordinary Shannon capacity and does not follow from a low per-trial error estimate. The paper's framing ("achievable rate established by exhibiting a working code," "cannot exceed the mutual information") is defensible, but it should name that it is estimating an empirical lower bound on the *zero-error* capacity per event, and that 8 exact recoveries is a sampling estimate, not an information-theoretic guarantee. *Fix:* one sentence naming zero-error capacity ($C_0 \le C_{\text{Shannon}}$) and clarifying the empirical status.

- **MINOR — Existence collapsing at the dirname rung is under-explained — Table 1 (line 387), §5.4.** `existence` drops to 0 at the *dirname* column (before the existence rung), which is initially surprising. The implicit reason — existence coding presupposes sender-chosen slot identities, so once file and directory names are both closed there are no distinguishable slots whose presence can encode bits — is sound but never stated. This dependency is load-bearing for the headline "only closing directory names reaches zero." *Fix:* state the existence-depends-on-names dependency explicitly where the table is discussed.

- **MINOR — Present an actual shared-resource matrix — §2.1 / §4.1.** The paper invokes Kemmerer's SRM as its analytic frame but never renders one (rows = attributes, columns = primitives, cells = R/M). Table 1 is the "modify/achievable-rate" projection only. A literal SRM would strengthen the classical grounding at near-zero cost and make the enumeration auditable. *Fix:* add a small SRM figure/table.

**Recommendation signal**

**Minor revision (accept with revisions).** The covert-channel framing is fundamentally sound, the co-tenant/cross-tenant distinction is correct, and the authors scope their own novelty honestly; the required fixes — engaging the IFC/DIFC literature to bound the central claim, committing to the "verification method" framing of the AIS-11 gap, adding a structural (not merely empirical) argument for the zero rung, and supplying ~3 canonical references (Myers–Liskov/HiStar, NCSC-TG-030, Ristenpart) — are all textual and need no new experiments. The one item that would escalate to Major at a stricter security venue is the IFC engagement, because it touches the defensibility of the headline novelty claim.
